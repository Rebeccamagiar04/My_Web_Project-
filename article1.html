Against the Odds: Real Stories of Students Who Turned Scholarships into Second Chances

Some students study by lantern light in refugee camps. Others work before school to pay fees, or hesitate to apply for a scholarship because they assume people like them are never chosen. The people in this article faced war, homelessness, poverty, and displacement, and each one won a scholarship. Their stories come from universities, newsrooms, and humanitarian agencies on four continents, and we have kept the details in, so you can see what each person actually did.

Kennedy Odede: the boy who started a movement with a soccer ball

Kennedy Odede grew up in Kibera, a slum in Nairobi often described as Africa's largest. He was the eldest of eight children and became a street child at ten. He had no formal schooling, yet he taught himself to read while living on the streets. A school place had once slipped away from him over money: his mother had saved three dollars over many months, not knowing the fees were ten.

In 2004, he was working in a factory, earning one dollar for ten hours of work. He saved twenty cents, bought a soccer ball, and started the youth group that grew into Shining Hope for Communities.

Then an American student named Jessica Posner arrived in Kibera for an internship, and the two began working side by side. When she returned to Wesleyan University in the United States, she pressed the admissions office on his behalf. His essays and letters of recommendation persuaded them to take a chance on someone who had never attended formal school. Wesleyan offered him a full scholarship.

He graduated in 2012. He was Wesleyan's commencement speaker and finished with honours in Sociology. Even before graduating, he and Posner used a $10,000 grant to build a school for girls in Kibera in the summer of 2009. It became the slum's first tuition-free school for girls. As a first-year student he told his university paper, "Education has saved my life."

Dawn Loggins: the school custodian who became a Harvard student

Dawn Loggins's childhood in North Carolina was unstable long before she was left alone. She moved often, sometimes squatting with her stepfather and mother, and she lived through stretches without power or water. Her stepfather was frequently arrested, and the family moved from place to place. Sometimes, her mother used the rent money to bail him out of jail, leaving the family struggling to keep a stable home. She and her younger brother sometimes walked twenty minutes to a public park to get water for cooking and flushing the toilet.

She kept doing well at school. At seventeen she attended a prestigious state academic programme for gifted students, and returned to find that her parents had left for Tennessee. Her grandmother was in a shelter, and her brother was staying with friends. Every call to her parents ended in a disconnected-number message.

She moved from couch to couch until a school counselor asked a custodian, Sheryl Kolton, to take her in. The school did not hand her over to the system either. Staff chose to provide for Dawn and her brother themselves. To support herself, Dawn worked as a custodian at her own high school, two hours before classes and two hours after. She took advanced placement and honours classes in rooms she had cleaned herself.

Once she had a safe home, she became president of the photography, rock climbing, and Spanish clubs, joined the National Honor Society and Beta Club, and started a community service project for soldiers. Looking back, she said that once she was allowed to join activities, "I just took off!" A friend urged her to aim high, and she applied to Harvard. When the acceptance came, it was addressed to the Harvard class of 2016.

She graduated from Burns High School on June 7, 2012, near the top of her class. She planned to pay for college with financial aid, local scholarships, and her savings from cleaning classrooms. She had watched neglect and hardship around her and decided she would not end up like her parents. Her message to others: "It depends on you and no one else." Sheryl Kolton, who took her in, saw it differently. She believes many people would have done the same if they had known the circumstances.

Sambo Dul: from a border camp to law school

Sambo Dul's earliest memories are of a makeshift camp. She lived four years in a camp on the Thai-Cambodian border before making an 8,000-mile journey to Arizona with her mother and three siblings at age five. Her father and two siblings had died.

In Arizona she attended Corona del Sol High School and then won full scholarships to Arizona State University, while continuing to work part time to support her family. Alongside her studies, she began volunteering with Amnesty International and soon founded Community Outreach & Advocacy for Refugees, training students to befriend and mentor newly settled refugee families.

The organisation grew quickly. It served more than 600 refugees, had over 70 volunteers and a 14-member board, developed a semester-long college preparation programme for refugee youth, and was preparing to expand to other campuses. Her grades were just as strong. She graduated Phi Beta Kappa in May 2005 with degrees in political science, economics, and Spanish, and won the ASU President's Scholarship, the Dorrance Merit Scholarship, and an entrepreneurship award along the way.

She then received one of the most generous awards in the United States, the Jack Kent Cooke Foundation Scholarship. It pays for an entire graduate education, based on achievement and financial need, with up to $50,000 a year for tuition, room, board, fees, and books for up to six years. She used it to attend law school at New York University.

Juliane Lukambo: ten years in Uganda, then a valedictorian's podium

Juliane Lukambo spent most of her childhood in exile. Her family fled war in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and spent ten years in Uganda before reaching the United States. Her American schooling began late, in the fifth grade at Valley Forge Elementary School, and the adjustment was not easy. She described arriving in a new country without knowing what to expect.

She took college classes while still in high school and stayed involved in school life, with a love of math, STEM Club, and the soccer and bowling teams. She graduated as valedictorian of Northland High School in Columbus, Ohio, with more than $240,000 in scholarships, and planned to study computer science at the University of Dayton. Even before college, she interned in the Ohio Department of Transportation's Smart Mobility Ambassador Program.

She said she never expected to reach this point, and told her classmates to "get out of your comfort zone." Her $240,000 came from many smaller awards, not one large one, which is a reason to apply to many scholarships rather than only one.

Henok Gheotom: a refugee who earned Cambridge's first refugee scholarship

Henok Gheotom came to the UK from Eritrea as a refugee and earned a First Class degree in Economics from the University of Bristol. He then applied for a scholarship that had only just been created. The Quantedge Foundation had set it up for refugee students to study an MPhil at King's College, Cambridge, supporting a student-led campaign that had worked since 2017 to widen access for people from conflict backgrounds.

In October 2020 he became the first recipient, studying an MPhil in Economics and Finance. The college highlighted two things about his application. One was his academic record and the recommendations from his tutors. The other was his dedication to helping disadvantaged students reach higher education. He backed this up. He became a mentor with The Access Project, which offers personalised tuition to disadvantaged students working toward top universities.

He graduated surrounded by his family, and said the scholarship was "nothing short of a blessing." He also said he could not have accepted his Cambridge offer without it. He now works in the energy sector on low-carbon solutions. The scholarship continues to support refugee students pursuing postgraduate study at Cambridge, opening doors for others who face barriers to higher education.

Doha Abualhija and Bayan Alhasani: two Palestinian students at Dundee

At the University of Dundee, in Scotland, two students received humanitarian scholarships. The university was recognised as a University of Sanctuary after welcoming dozens of students from war-torn regions in one year.

Doha is originally from Palestine. She and her four siblings moved with their parents to a refugee camp in Jordan, where she lived for fourteen years. She trained as a dentist and worked for three years in the camp. Describing her life, she said, "I've spent most of my life in a refugee camp." She came to Dundee in 2018 on a humanitarian scholarship for a master's in forensic dentistry and is now working on a PhD. She hopes to return to Jordan and encourage officials to accept dental evidence as a primary forensic identifier.

Bayan grew up in Gaza. His family home was bombed four times over twelve years, most recently in 2021, when it was partially destroyed. He earned a civil engineering degree anyway. His search for scholarships began at a bleak moment: the territory was under siege, a pandemic was under way, and he had lost his job. Much of his family's savings had gone into rebuilding the house, and he knew that without a scholarship, further study was unlikely. The answer arrived the same year, just as the 2021 war ended, accepting him onto a master's in sustainability and water security. He says he has been met with kindness since the day he arrived in Dundee. One university official described the goal as letting students fulfil their dreams and then give back to their communities.

Alaa: top of her department, studying by a solar lantern

Alaa was nineteen when the UN Refugee Agency told her story in 2015. Her family had moved to Za'atari camp in Jordan in January 2013 after their village in Syria's Dara'a province was destroyed. Space was tight, so she often studied outdoors in searing heat, which gave her heatstroke, or late at night by a solar lantern.

At al-Bayt University she finished first in her department in the end-of-year exams and posted the second-highest mark in the whole academic year. Her DAFI scholarship covered tuition, registration, transport, and books. DAFI, the Albert Einstein German Academic Refugee Initiative, is a UNHCR scholarship programme that helps eligible refugees access higher education. She called hearing that she had won it the happiest moment of her life. She also described what university meant beyond the classroom: leaving the camp meant seeing trees and colours other than desert.

Her story also shows how limited these scholarships can be. New DAFI scholarships in Jordan were dropping from 50 to 10 that year because of budget shortfalls, and only one student per family could qualify. Her younger sister Sondos, then seventeen, hoped to study medicine, and Alaa was ready to give up her own scholarship so Sondos could study. The camp manager refused to let that happen and promised to find another scholarship for Sondos. With so few places, it matters to apply early and to apply to more than one programme. Alaa said, "Education is the first block in building a strong society."

Zainab: thirteen years from displacement to a master's degree

The lecture hall at the University of Duhok fell silent as Zainab Abdullah finished defending her master's thesis, then erupted in applause. It had been thirteen years since her family fled Syria in 2013. Her mother had told her they were going to Iraq for one week, and carried one precious item: a folder of Zainab's school certificates.

The first years were painful. She was deeply depressed and cried almost daily, fearing she would never continue her education. Using her Syrian documents, she enrolled in high school under the region's refugee-inclusive policies and graduated with excellent grades. University still looked out of reach until a DAFI scholarship covered tuition and materials and provided a small living stipend. She studied Political Science at Duhok and graduated at the top of her class, feeling welcomed by teachers and classmates.

As a young mother living in Domiz refugee camp, she then enrolled in a master's degree, balancing childcare, housework, and study, and presented her thesis in October 2025. She described the scholarship as "like a ship that carried me toward my dreams." She plans to use her education to improve life in her community.

Ornella, Florine, and Mbabazi: three paths, one scholarship

Ornella Banam fled the Central African Republic for Burkina Faso in 2014, after rebels attacked the church where she worshipped. She kept studying, hoping to become a nutritionist, but dropped out when her father died and she could no longer afford the fees. Years later, a DAFI scholarship brought her back, covering her tuition, rent, and living expenses in Côte d'Ivoire. She said it "gave me back the hope I had lost." She grew up where malnutrition was widespread and wants to care for children suffering from it.

Florine Lutumba was eight when war forced her family from Congo. She has lived in Côte d'Ivoire for fifteen years, and earned her baccalaureate there. Her father's ill health then forced her to leave her studies to help the family earn money. She watched friends move ahead and chose not to dwell on it. She began baking and selling pastries with her younger sister in Abidjan, earned enough to pay for evening classes in Business Administration, and later received DAFI support for her tuition from 2021. She says the difficulties refugees face motivate her rather than discourage her, and hopes to lead a major company one day.

Mbabazi Mugemana has been a refugee since the 1994 Rwanda genocide. He credits DAFI with helping a talented high school student become a graduate. Over the next two decades he earned a master's degree, became a teacher, educational consultant, and community leader, and began a PhD. He points out that the programme also gave scholars training and coaching on life, enterprise, and finding work. Today he sits as an observer on DAFI selection panels in Cameroon.

Mohamed Abdi: studying while caring for family

Mohamed Abdi grew up in a refugee camp in Kenya and started high school without speaking English. At 23 he graduated from the University of Central Lancashire with a 2:1 in Media Production, the first in his family to do so, and also started a motivational coaching company. His biggest obstacle was balance: his father needs daily care, and he wanted his two younger sisters to have the support to focus on their own studies. He describes his drive as being "a voice for the voiceless," with plans for a master's in journalism.

What these students have in common

Conditions were far from ideal. Light, money, safety, and language were all missing at some point, and they kept studying anyway.

Other people made a difference. A custodian, a university, a foundation, a student visitor, a camp manager. Kennedy's and Henok's applications included recommendations, and Dawn's school staff took her in. If you are applying, ask a teacher or counsellor who knows your work to write for you.

Service counted alongside grades. Henok's college named his work helping disadvantaged students as one of two reasons for choosing him. Sambo founded an organisation for refugees, and Dawn led several school clubs. If you have helped in your community, include it in your application.

Several had to stop studying and come back. Ornella left school when her father died, Florine left to earn money when her father became ill, and Zainab spent years displaced before she could start university. All three returned through DAFI scholarships.

Many of them gave back. Kennedy built a school, Sambo supported newly arrived refugees, Henok mentored students, and Mbabazi sits as an observer on DAFI selection panels in Cameroon.

Scholarships can come from many sources. Juliane's $240,000 came from many awards, so it helps to apply to several.

Your turn

Starting from where these students did is hard, and applying will not guarantee a place. Places are limited, as the drop in DAFI awards in Jordan shows. Here is one way to begin this week:

  • Find one scholarship whose eligibility rules you meet, and write down its deadline.
  • Ask one teacher or counsellor whether they would write you a recommendation.
  • Write a short, honest paragraph about what you have done, at school or for other people.
