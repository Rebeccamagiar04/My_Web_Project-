How Will You Pay for It? Funding Your Education Beyond Scholarships

Getting into university and being able to afford it are two different challenges. A student may receive no scholarship, win an award that covers tuition but not living expenses, or discover that an apparently affordable programme comes with costs they had not considered. This guide explains how to calculate the shortfall, investigate additional funding, compare lower-cost options and decide whether a study plan is financially realistic.

1. The Real Cost of University

A university's tuition fee is only one part of the amount a student may need to complete a degree. Accommodation, food, transport, health insurance, course materials and immigration requirements can add considerably to the total. The balance depends on the country, city, institution, course and a student's individual circumstances.

Financial pressure does not necessarily disappear after enrolment. A 2023–2024 survey by the US-based Hope Center for Student Basic Needs (Download the report from Lumina Foundation⁠) collected responses from 74,350 students at 91 institutions across 16 US states. Among those respondents, 59% reported at least one form of food or housing insecurity, 41% reported food insecurity, 48% reported housing insecurity and 14% reported homelessness.

These figures describe that survey's participants, not all university students worldwide. The report is useful because it illustrates the financial pressures that can persist after students begin their studies; it should not be treated as a global estimate.

Costs to include in your budget

  • Tuition and compulsory fees: Course fees, registration, laboratory charges, examinations and other mandatory institutional fees.
  • Accommodation and utilities: University housing or rent, deposits, electricity, water and internet.
  • Food and personal necessities: Groceries, meals, toiletries, laundry and other essential expenses.
  • Travel and relocation: Local transport, international flights, luggage and travel to the institution.
  • Health and immigration: Insurance, visa or residence-permit charges, medical requirements and renewals where applicable.
  • Academic equipment: A laptop if required, textbooks, internet data, software and specialist equipment.
  • Initial and unexpected expenses: Housing deposits, arrival costs, document certification and a modest emergency reserve.

Some expenses occur once; others recur every month or academic year. Build a budget that reflects when each payment is due, not merely the total cost of a degree.

Low tuition does not always mean low overall cost

Germany is one example. Many public universities charge no general tuition fee for many degree programmes, although exceptions and additional charges apply. International students who require a German study visa must also demonstrate sufficient living funds. The official German government portal lists €11,904 as the minimum blocked-account amount for 2026 under the relevant route. A scholarship or accepted alternative proof may also satisfy the financial requirement.

This is a financial-proof requirement, not tuition and not a guarantee that every student's actual living expenses will be covered. Check the specific programme, state, institution and current visa rules before making a decision. Official German study-visa guidance.

2. Calculate Your Actual Funding Gap

Before searching for more money, work out exactly how much is missing. A funding gap is the amount of your expected education costs that is not covered by resources you can genuinely rely on.

Total costs for the period − confirmed available funding = funding gap

Use the same period for both sides of the calculation. If you calculate annual costs, compare them with funding available for that same year. If you calculate the whole degree, include future years and account for possible fee increases.

Count a scholarship only if you have received an official award and understand what it covers. Count family contributions only to the extent that the person has genuinely committed and can afford to provide them. Treat pending applications, hoped-for donations, unconfirmed jobs and possible loans as uncertain—not as money already available.

Separate your funding into three categories

  • Confirmed: Official awards, funds you already have and contributions that are firmly agreed and available by the payment deadline.
  • Applied for or possible: Scholarships awaiting a decision, bursaries not yet awarded, jobs you have not secured and fundraising that has not produced donations.
  • Unavailable or unverified: Funding for which you are ineligible, work you are not legally allowed to do, or money that cannot arrive before the payment deadline.

The first category determines whether you can currently afford to proceed. The second category can inform a future plan, but should not be treated as guaranteed income.

3. Start With the University

If you have already received an offer, the university is often the first organisation to contact. It knows the fee structure, its own financial-aid policies and whether any institutional support is available to students in your situation.

If you have not yet applied, investigate these possibilities before committing to an application or accepting an offer.

Ask specific questions about financial support

  • Need-based grants: Does the university assess financial circumstances, and are international applicants eligible?
  • Additional institutional funding: Can a student request a review of an award if the remaining cost makes enrolment impossible?
  • Tuition discounts or waivers: Are any reductions available for the particular course, student category or academic year?
  • Housing and meal support: Are there subsidised rooms, meal assistance or other benefits, and who qualifies?
  • Payment arrangements: Does the university permit instalments or an extension? Would an extension affect registration or immigration documentation?
  • Application and deposit waivers: Can eligible applicants request a waiver before paying?
  • Deferral: Can admission be postponed, and would the offer or financial aid remain valid?

Do not assume that a university offers every option on this list. Ask which arrangements actually exist and request the terms, amount and deadline in writing.

Understand what emergency grants are designed to do

Some institutions provide small grants to help enrolled students overcome an unexpected financial problem. These grants are not necessarily available to incoming students and may not cover large or recurring shortfalls.

For example, Georgia State University's Panther Retention Grant is designed to help eligible students who are approaching graduation and have a modest outstanding tuition or fee balance. The university identifies potential recipients; it is not a general grant that any applicant can request to finance an entire degree.

The lesson is to check the actual eligibility rules and purpose of a grant. A programme that helps an enrolled student finish their degree may not help someone pay an initial deposit, obtain a visa or travel to another country.

4. Look Beyond the University

If university aid is insufficient, look for other legitimate sources of support. Availability varies by country and personal circumstances, so investigate eligibility before spending money or time on an application.

Government funding and public bursaries

Some governments provide grants, loans, bursaries or subsidised education. Others reserve particular schemes for citizens, permanent residents or specific categories of residents. Eligibility can also depend on household income, academic progress, course type and the institution attended.

In Kenya, students can consult the Higher Education Financing portal, the Higher Education Loans Board and the National Government Constituencies Development Fund for information about their respective programmes.

However, do not assume that a refugee, non-citizen or international applicant qualifies simply because a scheme exists in the country where they live. Rules differ between programmes, and a funding scheme for Kenyan citizens may not be open to refugees. Ask the administering body about your exact legal and educational status before relying on the funding.

Charities, foundations and community organisations

Foundations, community organisations, religious charities, professional associations and refugee-support organisations sometimes offer education grants or referrals. Their support may be restricted by nationality, residence, field of study, age, displacement status or financial need.

Search for organisations that explicitly support students in your category. Read the eligibility criteria and funding conditions before applying. Small awards may help with books, transport or an initial fee, but they may not be sufficient to finance a full year of study.

Employer sponsorship

Some employers sponsor training related to their work or contribute to employees' education. This is most realistic when you already have a relationship with an employer or are applying to a structured training programme. Ask about obligations such as a minimum period of employment, repayment if you leave early, or restrictions on the course you can take.

An employer's verbal encouragement is not confirmed funding. Obtain a written agreement specifying the amount, payment schedule and conditions.

5. Can Work Help Pay the Bills?

Paid work can help some students cover living costs, gain experience or reduce the amount they need to borrow. But it should not automatically be treated as a solution to a large funding gap.

Check work permission before counting the income

The right to work depends on the country, immigration status, course and type of employment. Some student visas restrict working hours during term time, prohibit certain kinds of self-employment or limit work to approved placements. Rules can also differ for refugees, asylum-seekers and other residents whose status is not a student visa.

For example, Germany's official study-visa guidance sets conditions for employment by international students. In the United Kingdom, student visa work permissions depend on the course and the conditions attached to the visa. Consult the current official rules for your destination:

  • Germany: study visa and employment conditions
  • United Kingdom: Student visa guidance

These are examples, not universal rules. Always check the conditions that apply to your own status.

Understand the limits of student employment

  • You may not find a job immediately after arriving.
  • Available hours and wages may not be enough to pay tuition or satisfy financial requirements before arrival.
  • Jobs may be seasonal, competitive or unavailable to particular categories of students.
  • Working too many hours can affect attendance, assignments, health and academic progress.
  • A job can end, hours can be reduced and unexpected costs can absorb part of the income.

Calculate possible earnings only after checking legal permission, realistic hours, likely take-home pay and the cost of transport and other work-related expenses. Do not rely on a job you have not secured to satisfy a payment deadline that arrives before you can begin working.

Campus jobs that reduce expenses

Some universities offer residence-life, library, dining, research or student-support positions. Depending on the institution, a role may provide wages, accommodation, meals or a combination of benefits.

For example, Boston University's published residence-assistant information describes a specific combination of housing, meal benefits and a stipend for its designated roles. This is an institutional arrangement, not a standard benefit available at every university. Boston University residence-life role details.

Treat these positions as opportunities to investigate after checking eligibility, recruitment timing and the exact benefit. They are not guaranteed simply because you receive admission.

6. Consider Lower-Cost Pathways

If the funding gap remains large, it may be more effective to change the cost of the study plan than to keep searching for small amounts of money to cover an unaffordable programme.

Compare total costs, not tuition alone

A lower-tuition institution may be in a city with high rents, expensive transport or substantial insurance requirements. A programme with higher tuition may provide housing, meals or financial aid that makes the overall cost lower.

Compare the full annual cost for each option, including compulsory fees, living costs and initial expenses. Also consider whether the programme is recognised, whether it meets your career goals, and whether you can realistically meet its admission and language requirements.

The European Education Area explains that tuition varies by country, institution, programme and the applicant's status. Its guidance recommends checking tuition, living costs and available financial support together. European Education Area: managing your budget.

Tuition-free or subsidised public universities

Some public university systems have relatively low tuition fees, and some programmes charge no general tuition. This does not mean that all programmes are free or that every international student pays the same amount. Fees can depend on citizenship, degree level, subject, language and institution.

Check whether the course is taught in a language you can study in, whether you meet the entry requirements, and how you will meet living-cost and visa requirements. A tuition-free offer is not necessarily financially accessible if you cannot fund housing or the initial journey.

Online and distance-learning programmes

Distance learning can reduce relocation and accommodation costs, but compare the complete fee structure, connectivity requirements, assessment fees, equipment needs and recognition of the qualification. Make sure that the programme is suitable for the jobs or further study you intend to pursue.

The University of the People describes itself as a tuition-free institution, but students may still pay application and course assessment fees. Its 2026/27 undergraduate catalogue lists a $60 application fee and a $180 assessment fee per course under the standard published fee schedule. The estimated total for a Computer Science bachelor's degree completed entirely there is $7,260 under that schedule, before any additional applicable costs or changes. University of the People: 2026/27 estimated undergraduate fees.

These are published standard fees, not a guarantee that every student will pay the same amount. Check the current catalogue and ask the institution about financial assistance before assuming you can afford the programme.

Technical training and paid apprenticeships

In some countries, apprenticeships and work-based vocational programmes combine structured training with paid employment. They can offer an alternative to a conventional degree, but their availability, pay, entry requirements and qualification recognition differ significantly.

Check whether a programme is genuinely paid, whether the training provider is recognised, what qualification you receive, and whether you have permission to participate. Do not assume that an apprenticeship is available to all international applicants or that its salary will cover every living cost.

7. Reduce Costs Without Risking Your Studies

Reducing expenses can help close a modest gap. It is unlikely to solve a major shortfall if tuition and living costs are far beyond your available resources. Focus on costs you can change without compromising safety, health or academic progress.

Accommodation and food

  • Compare the full cost of university housing with safe, practical alternatives, including deposits, utilities and transport.
  • Consider shared accommodation where it is lawful, safe and suitable for your circumstances.
  • Compare meal plans with the realistic cost of buying and preparing food. Do not assume self-catering is cheaper everywhere.
  • Ask whether student services provide food assistance, emergency support or referrals to community services. Check eligibility and application dates.

Textbooks, software and equipment

  • Open textbooks: Search for legitimate open educational resources, including OpenStax. Its catalogue focuses on particular subjects and courses, so it may not replace every required textbook.
  • Library access: Check whether required readings are available through the university library or an authorised digital reserve.
  • Student software offers: The GitHub Student Developer Pack provides eligible students with access to selected developer tools and offers. Eligibility and individual offers vary.
  • Equipment: Ask whether the university lends laptops or provides computer labs before buying a device you may not need immediately.

Application costs

Before paying several application fees, build a shortlist of institutions that fit your academic qualifications and financial needs. Check official waiver policies, and ask whether a waiver must be approved before you submit the application.

A free application is not automatically an affordable university option. Consider the likely total cost and funding available before investing time in a programme that you cannot realistically attend.

8. Loans, Fundraising and Financial Risks

Borrowing is not the same as receiving a grant

A grant generally does not require repayment if the recipient follows its conditions. A loan does. Before accepting a loan, identify the interest rate, fees, repayment start date, repayment currency, consequences of missed payments and whether repayment is required if you do not complete the degree.

If repayment depends on income, check the precise contract. Do not assume that a loan is income-contingent, that repayments will be affordable, or that a lender will adjust payments when your circumstances change.

Borrowing can be particularly risky when a student has no reliable way to repay and expects that a degree will guarantee employment. No qualification guarantees a particular salary or a job immediately after graduation.

Crowdfunding and community contributions

Fundraising may help with a clearly defined cost, especially when a student can explain the funding gap, provide verifiable documentation and reach a community willing to contribute. However, donations are uncertain, may arrive late and can be affected by platform charges, transfer restrictions or the ability of supporters to give.

Do not base a required tuition deposit or visa financial requirement on donations that have not arrived. Protect personal documents, avoid publishing sensitive information unnecessarily and check how the fundraising platform handles fees, withdrawals and refunds.

Use a sensible order of preference

  1. Confirmed non-repayable grants and institutional aid.
  2. Affordable programmes and legitimate cost reductions.
  3. Verified employer sponsorship or other agreed support.
  4. Lawful employment, where the income and timing are realistic.
  5. Loans or fundraising only after examining their risks and uncertainty.

This is a planning framework, not a universal ranking. The best choice depends on the amount required, deadlines, eligibility and the conditions attached to each source.

9. When the Gap Is Too Large

Sometimes the amount missing is too large to cover through small bursaries, part-time work or savings on food and books. Recognising this early can prevent a student from accepting an offer that they cannot sustain.

Consider these options before committing

  • Request an aid review: Explain the shortfall to the university and ask whether additional support is possible. Do not assume a review will result in more funding.
  • Request a deferral: Ask whether admission can be postponed and whether the offer, scholarship or tuition rate would remain valid.
  • Compare other institutions: Search for a lower total cost, more comprehensive aid or a programme with a realistic funding package.
  • Consider a local or distance-learning option: This may reduce travel and relocation expenses, although tuition, internet access and equipment still need to be affordable.
  • Consider another entry cycle: Use the additional time to improve the funding plan, seek eligible support and apply to programmes with stronger financial packages.
  • Explore recognised technical training: A vocational qualification or paid training route may be more feasible, depending on the student's goals and local opportunities.
A crucial distinction: An admission offer proves that an institution is willing to admit you. It does not prove that you can afford to enrol or finish the degree. Before committing, check whether you can cover initial costs, meet any financial requirements, and sustain the plan in later semesters.

10. Two Funding Examples

The figures below are hypothetical illustrations, not estimates of typical university costs. They show how to calculate a gap; actual costs and funding opportunities differ by institution and country.

Example A: No scholarship

Illustrative annual cost: $10,000 (tuition and fees: $4,500; accommodation and food: $4,000; transport, books and other costs: $1,500).

Confirmed available resources: $1,500 (savings: $1,000; confirmed family contribution: $500).

Confirmed funding gap: $10,000 − $1,500 = $8,500.

What this means: The student should not assume that casual work or a few small bursaries will cover the entire $8,500. They should first ask the institution about aid and payment requirements, then compare lower-cost programmes and funding options. If no realistic combination covers the shortfall by the deadline, deferral or another programme may be the safer choice.

Example B: A partial scholarship

Illustrative annual cost: $14,000 (tuition and fees: $7,000; accommodation and food: $5,500; travel, books and other costs: $1,500).

Confirmed scholarship: $7,000, covering the stated tuition amount.

Confirmed funding gap: $14,000 − $7,000 = $7,000.

What this means: The student still needs to finance living expenses and other costs. They should ask whether the university offers housing assistance, additional grants or an aid review. They can also investigate legitimate work opportunities and external bursaries, but should not count them as secured funding until an offer is confirmed.

Test what happens if expected funding fails

Suppose the second student later receives a written award for $1,500. The remaining gap would fall from $7,000 to $5,500, assuming the award is available for the same year and can be used for the relevant costs.

If the student is also hoping to earn money from a campus job, they should make a separate estimate of possible earnings. That estimate is not confirmed funding until the job is secured and the likely income and start date are known. The student must decide whether they can proceed safely if the job does not materialise.

The purpose of a funding plan is not to make every number balance on paper. It is to establish whether the student can meet the actual deadlines and continue studying if one of the hoped-for sources of money falls through.

11. Build Your Step-by-Step Action Plan

  1. Calculate the total cost: Use the institution's published fees and a realistic budget for accommodation, food, travel, insurance and other compulsory expenses.
  2. Separate confirmed and possible money: Keep official awards and available funds separate from pending applications, possible jobs and hoped-for contributions.
  3. Contact the university: Ask specific questions about aid reviews, waivers, housing, payment deadlines and deferral.
  4. Check eligibility before applying: Read the official rules for each external grant, bursary or government scheme.
  5. Check work and immigration rules: Establish whether you can legally do the proposed work and when the income could realistically begin.
  6. Compare alternatives: Calculate the full cost of other institutions, locations, online programmes or recognised training routes.
  7. Write down every deadline: Include application dates, deposits, visa requirements, accommodation payments and tuition deadlines.
  8. Stress-test the plan: Ask what happens if a bursary is rejected, a job is delayed or an unexpected expense occurs.
  9. Get agreements in writing: Keep official award letters, employment agreements and approved payment arrangements.
  10. Make the decision before committing: Proceed only when the available funding and a credible plan for remaining costs meet the actual requirements.

12. Recognise Funding Scams

Financial pressure can make students vulnerable to people promising guaranteed scholarships, jobs, loans or visas. Treat an offer with caution if someone:

  • Guarantees admission, a visa or a scholarship in exchange for payment.
  • Demands an unexpected personal payment to release a grant or loan.
  • Pressures you to pay immediately or refuses to provide written terms.
  • Uses an unofficial email address while claiming to represent a university or government agency.
  • Requests account passwords, verification codes or unnecessary sensitive documents.
  • Asks you to transfer money through an untraceable method or to a personal account without a verifiable explanation.

Verify opportunities through the institution's official website and independently obtained contact details. Legitimate application or administrative fees do exist, so an upfront payment alone does not prove fraud. The important questions are whether the fee is official, documented, payable through an authorised channel and required under the published process.

Never pay an unverified person simply because they promise to secure an award. Keep copies of applications, official correspondence and payment receipts.

Before You Commit to a Study Plan

Financial access remains a major barrier to higher education, even when students have the academic qualifications to succeed. For refugees in particular, displacement and limited access to resources can make the gap between admission and enrolment especially difficult to overcome.

UNHCR reports that the global tertiary enrolment rate for refugees reached 11% in the 2024–2025 academic year, up from 9% in 2024. Its stated goal is to reach 15% by 2030. The figures show progress, but they also indicate that access to higher education remains limited for most refugees. UNHCR education data and resources.

There is no single funding method that works for every student. The useful starting point is to understand the full cost, identify the confirmed resources, investigate realistic additional support and compare alternatives before making a commitment.

Sometimes that process leads to an affordable plan. Sometimes it shows that an offer needs to be deferred or replaced with a more realistic option. Either outcome is better than committing to a degree on the assumption that uncertain money will eventually appear.
